\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Networked systems are exposed to an increasing volume and diversity of cyber threats. Modern enterprise traffic can contain millions of flow records within a single working day, creating a scale that is difficult to monitor through manual analysis alone. Traditional network intrusion detection systems (NIDS) primarily rely on signatures, rules, or manually defined indicators. Although these mechanisms remain effective for previously observed attacks, they are less capable of identifying unknown, obfuscated, or rapidly modified threats. Machine learning (ML) is therefore attractive for NIDS because it can learn discriminative patterns directly from traffic features and use them to classify suspicious behavior. By reducing dependence on manually maintained signatures, ML-based NIDS may adapt more readily to changing attack techniques and has consequently become a major direction in intrusion-detection research.

Over the past decade, researchers have proposed a wide range of supervised and unsupervised ML methods for this task. Supervised approaches, including tree-based ensembles and deep neural networks, learn from traffic samples with known labels, while unsupervised methods, such as autoencoders, attempt to identify deviations from learned patterns of normal behavior. Across these categories, many studies report very high accuracy, sometimes approaching perfect classification. Such results suggest that ML can provide powerful detection capabilities, but they do not necessarily provide a reliable basis for comparing methods. Reported performance depends not only on the model architecture, but also on the data, preprocessing decisions, evaluation protocol, and implementation details used in each study.

The comparability of existing results is limited by several recurring methodological differences. First, studies often use different datasets, or different cleaned, relabeled, and filtered versions of the same dataset. Even the two versions of CIC-IDS2017 considered in this work differ in their available fields, with 58 fields shared across the versions, making version choice itself a potential source of variation (Source: \texttt{docs/schema\_diff.md}). Second, evaluation protocols are inconsistent: some studies emphasize accuracy, whereas others report F1-score, recall, or area under the precision-recall curve. This matters in imbalanced datasets, where a classifier can obtain a high accuracy score by favoring the majority class. In the present data, benign traffic accounts for 83.1\% of samples in the original version and 75.4\% in the revised version (Source: \texttt{tables/class\_distribution.csv}). Third, studies apply different strategies for class imbalance, including no correction, random oversampling, undersampling, or synthetic data generation. Fourth, data leakage can inflate results through inappropriate feature selection or through duplicated flows appearing in both training and test sets. In the supplied logs, 2,830,751 raw records are reduced to 2,520,798 after cleaning, including the removal of 307,078 duplicate rows (Source: \texttt{logs/clean\_shturl}). Finally, code, preprocessing scripts, and hyperparameters are often unavailable, making independent verification difficult. Together, these differences weaken the meaning of cross-paper comparisons and make reported performance gaps difficult to interpret.

This study addresses this problem through a controlled comparison under a common experimental setting. Three representative methodological families are evaluated using the same data source, preprocessing pipeline, and train-test protocol: a supervised tree-based model from the gradient-boosting family, supervised deep-learning models based on one-dimensional convolutional or recurrent architectures, and an unsupervised autoencoder. The evaluation explicitly accounts for class imbalance by combining accuracy with class-balanced measures and precision-recall analysis, rather than relying on a single aggregate score. To reduce leakage, fields with potential target or evaluation contamination are excluded, records are deduplicated after cleaning, and data are partitioned at the flow level. The original and revised CIC-IDS2017 versions are then analyzed as a sensitivity comparison, allowing the stability of model rankings to be examined under dataset changes.

The contributions are threefold. First, we define and apply a leakage-aware, class-balanced evaluation protocol for ML-based NIDS. Second, we systematically compare supervised tree models, supervised deep models, and unsupervised representation learning under the same protocol. Third, the original-versus-revised dataset comparison shows how data quality and schema differences can affect measured performance and model ranking. Section~2 reviews related work, Section~3 describes the datasets and methodology, Section~4 presents the results, Section~5 discusses their implications and limitations, and Section~6 concludes the paper.
