# P7 Deliverables: Results §4.4 and Discussion

## Results §4.4: Cross-method comparison and corrected-data rerun

We compared the three main methods on the shared original/stratified/main Test split of 346,544 flows. All summary values are the mean ± sample standard deviation across seeds 42–46. The comparison uses continuous-score Average Precision (AP) and ROC-AUC, empirical Test ROC operating points, and attack-family recall. Threshold-dependent classification results remain tied to each method's Validation-calibrated decision rule and are not substituted for empirical ROC operating points.

GBDT G05 achieved the strongest ranking performance, with AP 0.9995 ± 0.0001 and ROC-AUC 0.9999 ± 0.0000. The 1D-CNN achieved AP 0.9172 ± 0.0233 and ROC-AUC 0.9809 ± 0.0040, while Autoencoder A06 achieved AP 0.7427 ± 0.0062 and ROC-AUC 0.9040 ± 0.0031. Figure 1 reports these five-seed summaries. Because complete precision–recall arrays were not available locally for every method, AP is shown as the common precision–recall summary instead of drawing incomplete or reconstructed curves. <!-- Sources: results/m1_test_summary/summary.json; results/m2.json; results/m3_summary/summary.json; figures/fig_p7_overall_performance.png -->

The same ordering remained at low false-positive-rate operating points. At an empirical Test FPR target of 1%, mean TPR was 1.0000 ± 0.0000 for GBDT, 0.6115 ± 0.0700 for the 1D-CNN, and 0.5272 ± 0.0164 for the autoencoder. At a 5% target, the respective TPR values were 1.0000 ± 0.0000, 0.7951 ± 0.0986, and 0.6177 ± 0.0138 (Figure 2). These empirical ROC quantities must not be confused with Test TPR and FPR obtained by applying a threshold frozen on Validation. <!-- Sources: results/p7_plot_values.csv; figures/fig_p7_tpr_at_fpr.png -->

Attack-family recall exposed differences that aggregate metrics concealed (Figure 3). Under the low-FPR rules used for the comparison, GBDT detected almost every flow in all 14 attack families. The 1D-CNN performed relatively well on DDoS (mean recall 0.69), DoS Hulk (0.85), FTP-Patator (0.62), and Heartbleed (0.80), but mean recall was zero for botnet, infiltration, and the three Web Attack categories. The autoencoder was strongest on DoS Hulk (0.84), DoS Slowloris (0.57), and Heartbleed (1.00), but failed to detect FTP-Patator, infiltration, portscan, SSH-Patator, Web Attack Brute Force, and SQL injection at its q99 rule. Heartbleed, infiltration, and SQL injection contain only 2, 5, and 3 Test flows, respectively, so their observed recalls are not stable estimates of rare-family generalization. <!-- Sources: results/p7_plot_values.csv; figures/fig_p7_family_recall.png -->

At the frozen low-FPR rules, mean Accuracy/Macro-F1 were 0.9925/0.9878 for GBDT, 0.9211/0.8478 for the 1D-CNN, and 0.9050/0.8074 for the autoencoder (Figure 4). These values describe different method-specific Validation-calibrated rules—GBDT and CNN at 1% and the autoencoder at q99—so they complement rather than replace the empirical ROC comparison. <!-- Sources: results/p7_plot_values.csv; figures/fig_p7_threshold_metrics.png -->

The corrected-data full rerun was reported as successfully completed on a separate computer using the frozen configurations. This checkout also contains successful corrected-data smoke evidence for M1, M2, and M3. However, the corrected full-run metric exports were not synchronized into the repository at integration time. ## Discussion

### Comparative effectiveness

The results favour GBDT for this intrusion-detection setting. Its near-perfect AP, ROC-AUC, and low-FPR TPR were consistent across five seeds, and its attack-family recall remained uniformly high. This outcome is plausible because the 140 inputs are engineered tabular flow features. Axis-aligned and interaction-based tree splits can exploit heterogeneous feature scales and nonlinear thresholds directly, whereas the local connectivity imposed by a 1D convolution assumes an ordering and neighbourhood structure that the feature vector does not naturally possess.

The 1D-CNN nevertheless provided strong overall discrimination, especially in ROC-AUC, but it was less reliable at strict low-FPR operating points and varied more across seeds. In an operational detector, the difference between high global ranking performance and moderate TPR at 1% FPR is important: a model may rank most attacks above most benign flows while still missing a substantial fraction of attacks when the alert budget is tightly constrained. The per-family analysis further shows that aggregate AP can hide complete blind spots for small or structurally distinct attack families.

The autoencoder addressed a different learning problem. It was fitted only to benign training traffic and therefore did not require attack labels, but reconstruction error was a weaker separator than the supervised scores. Its q95/q99 results demonstrate the expected precision–recall trade-off: a stricter benign-error quantile reduces false alarms and increases precision while lowering attack recall. The method may still be useful as a complementary anomaly detector for distributional novelty, but these experiments do not demonstrate that it generalizes to unseen attacks, and its zero recall for several known families cautions against using it as the sole detector.

### Thresholds and operational interpretation

Threshold-free metrics and deployed operating points answer different questions. AP and ROC-AUC summarize score ranking, while a Validation-calibrated threshold determines the realized Test FPR, TPR, precision, and Macro-F1. The empirical TPR@FPR values in Figure 2 are calculated from each Test ROC for descriptive cross-method comparison; they are not thresholds that were reselected for deployment. This distinction prevents Test information from leaking into threshold calibration and should be retained in the final manuscript and table captions.

At low FPR, GBDT's advantage is practically meaningful because false alarms can dominate analyst workload in high-volume traffic. However, the dataset is highly imbalanced and contains very small supports for several families. A recall of 1.00 based on two Heartbleed flows or five infiltration flows should not be interpreted as robust coverage. Future evaluation should add confidence intervals or grouped resampling at the traffic-session level and should test temporal or cross-dataset generalization.

### Efficiency and reproducibility

Mean recorded training time was approximately 500 seconds for GBDT G05, 1,929 seconds for the 1D-CNN, and 348 seconds for Autoencoder A06. These figures were collected on different hardware and have method-specific timing scopes, so they describe the delivered runs rather than a controlled speed benchmark. The CNN also required a CUDA-capable GPU for its formal training, whereas the tree model was trained under CPU resource controls. The autoencoder's low batch inference cost is attractive, but computational efficiency does not offset its lower detection coverage in this experiment.

Reproducibility was strengthened by fixed train/Validation/Test splits, seeds 42–46, frozen configurations, Validation-only model selection and threshold calibration, preserved per-seed outputs, and explicit source paths in `results/summary.csv`. The corrected-data rerun retained the frozen model settings and changed only the dataset version. Before quantitative original-versus-corrected sensitivity claims are added, the external corrected metric files should be synchronized, hashed, and processed by the same P7 aggregation script. This is a data-availability step rather than a reason to rerun the original experiments.

### Limitations

Four limitations should qualify the comparison. First, the corrected full-run metrics were not locally available at integration time, so dataset-version deltas are not yet reported. Second, M2's delivered operating points retain previously documented tie-handling differences from the P3 evaluator, although its AP and ROC-AUC were independently recomputed and matched. Third, raw precision–recall arrays were unavailable for all three methods in one common local format, so Figure 1 uses AP rather than fabricated curves. Fourth, evaluation on one stratified split does not establish temporal robustness, resilience to dataset shift, or detection of genuinely unseen attack families.

Overall, the evidence supports GBDT G05 as the strongest primary detector on the current tabular benchmark, the 1D-CNN as a competitive but more resource-intensive supervised alternative, and the autoencoder as a complementary anomaly-scoring method whose operating threshold must be selected according to the acceptable false-alarm budget.
